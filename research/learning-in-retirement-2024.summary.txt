In this November 2024 Learning in Retirement lecture (Part II of the UC Berkeley Retirement Center's "Berkeley in Space" series, given over Zoom on Election Day), Bloom explains for a general audience how machine learning became central to time-domain astronomy. He frames the "Rumsfeldian" challenge of finding the known and the unknown in surveys whose nightly transient counts grow from the Palomar Transient Factory through ZTF to Rubin's LSST, far outstripping follow-up telescopes; gives a primer on supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning; and maps where AI helps: midstream, downstream and upstream of the data. Midstream: the real-bogus discovery engine that flagged the Pinwheel Galaxy's Type Ia supernova 11 hours after explosion, whose rapid follow-up ruled out ordinary stars as its progenitor; the new La Silla Schmidt Southern Survey (LS4); self-supervised autoencoders and multimodal models that classify variable stars from few labels, rediscover known classes and surface mislabels and oddballs; a probabilistic catalog that tripled the known DY Per stars; physics-aware network architectures; and simulation-based inference for exoplanet microlensing, 100,000 times faster, which revealed an "offset degeneracy" that had been hiding in the lens equation for 50 years. He closes with downstream LLM summaries and embedding-based similarity search in SkyPortal and upstream ideas (deepCR cosmic-ray removal, reinforcement learning for adaptive optics, Rubin's active optics, and weather sensing for survey scheduling), ending with Oscar Wilde's "some of us are looking at the stars."
