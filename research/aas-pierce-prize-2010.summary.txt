In his January 2010 Newton Lacy Pierce Prize lecture at the AAS meeting in Washington, introduced by AAS president John Huchra, Bloom reviews gamma-ray bursts in three parts. First the phenomenology: sub-second variability, non-thermal prompt spectra, the short-hard versus long-soft split, panchromatic synchrotron afterglows and jetted energetics near 10^51 erg, and how Swift's early light curves, X-ray flares, plateaus and missing jet breaks have pushed the standard fireball model toward ad hoc complexity. Then the progenitors, in order of security: extragalactic magnetar flares (about 1% of bursts), long bursts from collapsing massive stars with broad-line Ic supernovae (Woosley's collapsar), the supernova-less long bursts 060614 and 060505 ("Woosley's biggest blunder"), the Galactic oddity 070610, and short bursts tied to early-type hosts and compact-object mergers, including GRB 080503's day-long rebrightening as a candidate Li-Paczynski mini-supernova. Last, their utility: as the brightest lighthouses they probe host galaxies through damped Lyman-alpha spectra and the luminosity-metallicity relation, measure extragalactic dust (including supernova-made dust near redshift 5), show that few dark bursts lie at very high redshift, and can probe reionization (GRB 090423 at redshift 8.2, the proposed EXIST mission), while short bursts seen with advanced LIGO could pair luminosity distance with redshift for a new cosmology.
