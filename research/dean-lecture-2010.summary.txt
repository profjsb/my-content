In this October 2010 Benjamin Dean Lecture in the Morrison Planetarium, Bloom argues that astronomy is undergoing a phase transition: seen with enough sensitivity, everything in the sky changes, and the data now arrive faster than astronomers can look at them. He starts from Galileo's notebook of Jupiter's moons as a time-domain observation made by humans in the loop, and tours the dynamic universe (SN 1987A, the discovery movie of Eris, Fomalhaut b, the parts-per-million "burbling" of stars seen by Kepler) as "celestial cinematography", then the three intellectual thrusts of transient science (introspection, exploitation, exploration) with pulsars, gamma-ray bursts, his SDSS search for eclipsing M dwarfs, Leavitt's Cepheids and supernovae. The technology follows: CCDs, robotic telescopes such as his PAIRITEL that respond to Swift alerts within a minute and tweet what they observe, VOEvent messages that let telescopes talk to each other, and the Palomar Transient Factory's 1.5 million candidates a night, sifted with crowdsourcing and machine learning. Classifying variable stars from noisy, irregularly sampled light curves exposes a messy taxonomy, yet a classifier's confusion matrix rediscovers physical kinships between classes, and he closes with Tycho's supernova and Hamlet. In the Q&A he discusses Kepler's data deluge, how PTF names its discoveries, a 3D map of the Milky Way and neutron-star mergers as gravitational-wave sources, robotizing small telescopes, computational training for astronomy students, the public's connection to the night sky, and data sharing.
