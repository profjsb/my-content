In this September 2012 Benjamin Dean Lecture in the Morrison Planetarium, introduced by Ryan Wyatt, Bloom tells the story of SN 2011fe, "the supernova of a generation." He opens with Hamlet as an allegory for the Copernican revolution and Tycho's supernova of 1572, then tours historical supernovae (SN 1054 and the Chaco Canyon pictograph, SN 1987A) and their remnants on the planetarium dome, the elements forged in the explosions ("you are nuclear waste"), and the difference between core-collapse and Type Ia supernovae. Type Ia supernovae as calibrated standard candles lead to Hubble's law, the accelerating universe behind the 2011 Nobel Prize to Perlmutter, Schmidt and Riess, and dark energy ("levity"), even though no one yet knows what makes them explode. The Palomar Transient Factory's robotic telescope and machine-learned real-bogus codes, which sift 1.5 million candidates a night, caught SN 2011fe in the Pinwheel Galaxy about 11 hours after explosion; Hubble's pre-explosion images and the smooth early light curve ruled out red-giant and helium-star companions, showing that an exploding white dwarf makes a Type Ia supernova, while PTF11kx's red-giant companion points to a second channel. The long Q&A covers the distance ladder and the Phillips relation, late-time spectra and light echoes of historical supernovae, how the real-bogus classifier is trained, measuring the Hubble constant, dark energy, and anomaly detection.
