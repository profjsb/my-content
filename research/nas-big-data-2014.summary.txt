At the National Research Council workshop "Training Students to Extract Value from Big Data" (April 11, 2014), Bloom argues that domain scientists now need a bewildering toolkit that graduate training rarely provides, a need that is pressing in astronomy, where LSST, LOFAR and SKA bring a data deluge and his group's machine-learning pipeline already finds transients in real time. He recounts Berkeley's Python boot camps (85 campers in 2010; open materials reused by NASA Goddard) and the follow-on graduate seminar, which drew about 40 for-credit students once "data" entered its title and whose alumni include the lead author of a PNAS Kepler study. Then the "dirty laundry": where such courses fit in curricula, whether they are too vocational, and how to credit and employ the people who teach them. He advocates data literacy (statistical inference, versioning and reproducibility, the IPython Notebook) before big-data proficiency, collaborations rather than unicorn data scientists, a way past the "novelty-squared" problem, and inclusive environments: about a third of boot-campers were women, against a handful at AMP Camp and two of the workshop's 22 speakers.
