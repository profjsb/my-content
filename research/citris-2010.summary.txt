In this March 2010 CITRIS Research Exchange lecture, introduced by Masoud Nikravesh, Bloom uses Galileo's notebook of Jupiter's moons to frame astronomy as a time-domain science that has always kept humans in the loop, a role now changing by necessity. He tours the dynamic universe (the discovery images of Eris, Fomalhaut b, eclipsing M-dwarf binaries mined from SDSS, supernovae and rarer, faster explosions) and the technologies transforming how data are taken: CCDs, robotic telescopes such as KAIT and his own PAIRITEL, and the VOEvent standard that lets telescopes announce events to each other. Discovery engines like the Palomar Transient Factory, and soon LSST, invert the old problem by producing far more discoveries than follow-up resources, so his Transients Classification Project applies machine learning throughout: a real-bogus score to sift PTF's 1.5 million nightly candidates, features drawn from light curves and sky context, the DotAstro.org light-curve repository, and classifiers whose confusion matrices rediscover the physical kinship of variable-star classes. He closes with open problems (learning with uncertain features, building in domain knowledge, parallelization, classifiers that weigh the cost of being wrong) and the rich interface between astronomy, statistics and computer science.
