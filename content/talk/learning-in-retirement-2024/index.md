How machine learning became central to discovery and inference in time-domain astronomy (real-bogus supernova discovery, self-supervised variable-star models, and simulation-based inference that uncovered a new microlensing degeneracy) and where AI goes next: downstream in AI-guided exploration, upstream in telescopes run as robots.

*Second of three Tuesday lectures in "Berkeley in Space", a series organized by Donald Mastronarde for the UC Berkeley Retirement Center's Learning in Retirement program (Oct 29 – Nov 12, 2024). Given over Zoom on Election Day; the recording ends before the Q&A.*

## Key Quotes

> "We don't have personally identifiable information in our data. It's not that valuable. And if you make a mistake, you don't make a billion-dollar error. Maybe you're embarrassed in your career or something like that." – Joshua Bloom

> "It's fast, parallelizable, transparent, deterministic, and versionable — pretty much none of which is the characteristics you would describe of a person." – Joshua Bloom

> "I, for one, don't like to do machine learning just because it's fun. It is fun, but it's actually quite painful and hard, and to do it right requires a lot of computation and a lot of careful and expert knowledge. We do machine learning in astronomy because we're trying to get at new science." – Joshua Bloom

> "This degeneracy that we discovered using machine learning is actually deeply embedded in the math of the so-called gravitational lens equation, and it had really been hiding in plain sight for the last 50 years." – Joshua Bloom

> "It's not just about using AI to accelerate discovery of the known knowns, or even help us find anomalies of actual objects. This is now giving us a deeper theoretical view of how the universe works." – Joshua Bloom
