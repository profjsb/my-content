Pierce Prize lecture on gamma-ray bursts: a phenomenology that has outgrown the standard fireball model, a widening cast of progenitors (extragalactic magnetars, collapsing massive stars, long bursts without supernovae, and compact-object mergers, with the hunt for a Li–Paczyński mini-supernova), and bursts as tools: lighthouses for distant galaxies and their dust, probes of reionization, and electromagnetic partners to gravity-wave events.

*The [2009 Newton Lacy Pierce Prize](https://aas.org/grants-and-prizes/newton-lacy-pierce-prize-astronomy) lecture, given in Invited Session 103 (Marriott Ballroom, 3:40–4:20 pm) and introduced by John Huchra, the AAS president, who read the prize citation; the session ran out of time for questions. The video is the AAS's own recording, listed on the meeting page.*

## Abstract

*AAS abstract [103.01](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2010AAS...21510301B/abstract).*

While the majority of gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) have been established as due to the death of massive stars, there is increasing evidence that many other progenitor systems — from degenerate binary coalescence to extragalactic magnetars — are responsible for some small fraction of observed events. Understanding how this diverse set of physical origins are manifest in the high-energy phenomenology is an ongoing pursuit, drawing interesting parallels to supernova taxonometric work. Yet, irrespective of classification, GRBs are emerging as unique tools in other aspects of astronomy. I will review some of these promising new directions, including the connection to the gravity-wave universe and the use of GRBs to probe the epoch of reionization.
